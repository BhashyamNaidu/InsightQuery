# Seasonal and Temporal Crime Patterns: General Context

Criminological research across many U.S. cities, including Chicago, has repeatedly
observed a seasonal pattern often called the "summer peak": many crime categories,
particularly violent street crime, robbery, and certain property crimes, tend to rise in
warmer months (roughly May through September) and decline in colder months. The leading
explanatory framework is "routine activity theory" — warmer weather increases the amount of
time people spend outdoors and in public spaces, which increases the opportunities for
both victimization and offending, independent of any change in the underlying population
disposed to commit crime.

This pattern is not uniform across offense types. Categories tied to indoor activity or
opportunistic property access — certain burglary and some fraud/deception categories — show
weaker or even inverse seasonal patterns compared to street-level violent and property
crime. Holiday periods (particularly the winter holiday season) also show localized spikes
in specific categories like retail theft and certain fraud categories, independent of the
broader seasonal temperature effect.

Day-of-week and time-of-day patterns are similarly well documented: many violent crime
categories skew toward weekend and nighttime hours, while certain property and white-collar
offense categories skew toward weekday business hours when the relevant targets (offices,
commercial deliveries) are active.

These are general, well-established directional patterns from the broader criminological
literature, not Chicago-specific measured figures for any particular year — any claim about
"this year's" seasonal pattern for a specific crime type should be checked directly against
that year's monthly data rather than assumed from this general context alone.
