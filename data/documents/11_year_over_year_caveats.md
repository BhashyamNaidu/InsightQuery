# Methodological Pitfalls in Year-Over-Year Crime Comparisons

Comparing crime counts across years is one of the most requested and most easily
mis-stated forms of analysis, because several structural factors can move the numbers
independent of any real change in criminal activity.

**Classification changes.** Offense classification standards are periodically revised by
the Illinois State Police and the FBI. The most significant recent example nationally was
the shift from the FBI's older Summary Reporting System to the incident-based NIBRS
standard (federally mandated starting in 2021), which changed how multi-offense incidents
are counted. Even without a national standards change, individual IUCR code definitions or
primary-type groupings can be revised, which can create a step change in a category's count
that is purely definitional, not behavioral.

**Reporting-lag asymmetry.** Because recent months are systematically undercounted at the
time of data extraction (see the data-quality-limitations document) and settle upward over
following weeks, comparing "this year's most recent month" to "the same month last year,"
which has had a full year to settle, will structurally understate the current year unless
both periods are pulled with equivalent lag.

**Population and exposure changes.** A raw incident count does not account for changes in
population, foot traffic, or the number of reporting opportunities (e.g., a new large
retail development can mechanically increase retail-theft counts in its community area
without reflecting any change in underlying safety).

**Base-rate sensitivity in small denominators.** Percentage changes computed on
low-frequency categories (e.g., arson, homicide in a small community area) can look
dramatic — "up 200%" — from a shift of only a handful of incidents, and should always be
reported alongside the absolute counts, not the percentage alone.

The defensible practice is to report absolute counts alongside any percentage change,
note the extraction date and any reporting-lag adjustment applied, and flag when a
comparison spans a known classification-standard change.
